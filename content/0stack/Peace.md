There's an orientation towards the world that I call "peacefulness" that is important to me. Peace is a bit of a confusing word for this concept, because there are things that don't superficially look "peaceful" which fit my definition of peace. I aspire to always be at peace in the sense that I'll try to point to in this post.

> Note: The claim that I always aspire towards peace as gestured at in this post may be controversial. I'm interested in chatting with people about this, and am open to being convinced that peace is not always desirable. 

Here are some **non**-examples of peace I've noticed in myself and others recently:
- Not accepting an unpleasant reality.
	- After hurting someone, you pursue flaily attempts at making them feel better, or make lots of apologies, potentially even turning into a problem for them to deal with.
	- After hurting someone, you desperately want them to stop feeling hurt.
	- When someone is feeling hurt, offering [[Against premature help spaghetti|help spaghetti]].
- Un-carefulness.
	- Being a bit overly free and uncareful with giving compliments or gratitude, such that sometimes it's not quite accurate.
	- Giving a quick off the cuff answer to someone, probably a short message, rather than sitting down and contemplating your paragraph a bit. (Without there being a good reason for why to give this type of response).
	- Not knowing what you're going to say in advance, just making it up on the spot. (Without this being appropriate; eg this wouldn't indicate lack of peace if you were doing improv).
- Social interactions.
	- Having a social interaction where we're saying things a little bit too fast, like it's almost like we're scared of silence. The social interaction probably involves us talking loudly. To be clear sometimes this is totally fine and/or desirable. But sometimes it feels bad.
	- Having a conversation where you interrupt each other a lot, without this being an established norm that both people clearly feel good about.
	- Having a superficial social interaction; it could've been sincere, but wasn't.
- Disagreements. 
	- Being upset and flustered in disagreements. Having difficulty seeing other perspectives.
	- Being kind of impatient with others. Repeating things in your ontology when you think someone is wrong.
These might seem pretty disparate but to me they feel like related to one class of mental states.

Here's what peace looks like instead: 
- Sitting with unpleasant realities. Prioritize listening and understanding how the world is over changing it. Actions taken without a decent understanding of how the world is may backfire.
	- If your friend is feeling sad, first listen to them instead of trying to get them to cheer up.
	- If your friend is feeling like they messed up, don't tell them you don't think it's a big deal, listen to them.
	- If you've hurt your friend, don't make an apology that's all about you and your faults. Prioritize listening to them and understanding how they're feeling.
	- Peace involves not expecting or asking for forgiveness. There is only prediction not trust, and the experience is a useful data point in helping us make calibrated predictions about the future.
	- Peace involves apologizing **simply** when you're sorry. Simply does involve being precise.
	- Expressing [[Expressing and receiving appreciation|gratitude]] **simply** and in a way that conveys your emotions.
- Carefulness.
	- Being judicious in when to give compliments. Don't give them thoughtlessly, give them after deliberation. Be precise.
	- Write a careful response to someone. This involves responding at a time when you actually have the ability to give a well-thought-out response.
	- Clarifying whether the epistemic status of a thing you're saying is "random speculation" or "well thought out opinion".
- Social interactions.
	- Have pauses / allow for some silence without anxiety in conversations.
	- Generally, don't interrupt people.
	- Sincerity.
- Disagreements. 
	- Prioritize listening to others in a disagreement, even if this feels like the slower route.
		- Spelling out your own ontology is fine if they are actually into you doing this.

Here are some cases that where the label of peace might seem a bit weird:
 - I enjoy being ecstatically excited about a math problem in a way that invokes much more generation than [[Problem Solving With a Generator and Refuter|pruning]]. Is that peaceful? I think yes, as long as I know that this is what I'm doing. 
 - How to feel in the face of the death of a loved one?
	 - I endorse feeling angry at the universe.
	 - I endorse accepting rather than denying this reality.
	 - I think feeling extreme sadness is appropriate. 
	 - Peace is about being emotionally connected to reality.
	 - Peace might involve having a 4D perspective on life, after their death reliving some memories, trying to enact the deceased's desires.
	 - Anyways, the mental state I've described here is currently the one I'm least confident is well described as peace. But I tentatively think it is still peace.

How can one achieve peace?
- Speak more deliberately and a tad slower, with a thoughtful pause before you speak.
- Prioritize listening to others.
- Try to understand bad world states before fixing them (to the extent that this makes sense).
- I have some general non-public guidelines on "how to want" that seem quite applicable. I am copying part of that post over to this post.

# Appendix: How to want 
# 1. Want the right thing
## 1a. Focus wanting on through conditionals 
Often I'll say things like "I want to do something good". Unfortunately, by default this might mean "I want to X such that Alek-thinks-is-good(X) = true". That's susceptible to hacking Alek.

When you use fallible proxies to try to achieve your goal, it's good to recognize that, and head it off. You must recite:
- I want to be right, not just feel right. If I'm wrong, then I want to notice that. Smile when you realize you're wrong, laugh, positively reinforce yourself a bit.
- When you've hurt someone, you want to understand it. Feeling overly bad about it isn't very helpful immediately. Apologizing after the fact is better.
- Love critical feedback. Congratulate yourself on receiving it with composure. Have a prior on the feedback being correct! Extract as much value as you can from the feedback. 

When you realize that you're having an instinctual negative response to learning that the world did not turn out to be the way you wanted, notice that and take 3 deep breaths.
## 1b. Sometimes, you should not want
- Sometimes there are things that you know are really bad for you, but you still want them anyways because they fulfill a proxy goal, or are myopically good. In such situations, notice this, and stop wanting it.
- For instance, it might be productive to explicitly not want a bad romantic relationship.
## 1c. Be attached to processes, not outcomes.
- Give yourself partial credit. If you negatively reinforce yourself for halfway achieving goals, for doing things imperfectly, that makes it a bunch rougher to enjoy doing them. 
 - Look for the ways that you're partially succeeding and celebrate those.
- Spelling out processes specifically.
	- Do not say, "I want to treat my friend kindly" instead say "I want to be proactive in thinking of nice things to do for my friend, I want to be proactive in looking for faults in my approach to caring for my friend".
- I think wanting good process is especially good when you cannot control the final outcome.
## 2. Find peace with the fact that you did not get what you wanted
- Choose to realize that it's common and not surprising for things to not go the way you wanted.
- Focus on the fact that there is still some good in the world.
- Don't just focus on the things that didn't go the way you wanted, focus on the things that went well too.
## 3. See foiled want as a chance to be virtuous
Here are some things that I think are really cool: 
- Cooperating after someone has defected.
- Owning up to your mistakes.
- Moving on when you've been hurt.
- Being forgiving about things.
	- Note: being forgiving about things doesn't give other people license to walk over you. You can also be assertive.
- Deciding not to give up.
