Kahneman claims that the remembered valence of a memory depends largely on the peak valence and the final valence. For instance, people remember a surgery more fondly if you add a period of mild discomfort at the end of it! IIUC Kahneman came to this hypothesis via mildly torturing people in different ways and seeing which ones they preferred (and testing some causal interventions).[^1]

Ultimately, I care about the aggregate quality of observer moments in the universe, not the quality of my memories. However, memories are a hugely important part of my experience! Memory is the reason why N can say "cocoa powder beans" and I'll burst out laughing. It's why I smile when I see an orange (oranges have existed in dozens of happy contexts with P).

Therefore, to maximize the quality of my subjective experience it is expedient to understand how to have good memories. This post, loosely inspired by Kahneman's analysis, will think about how to do this.

# Keeping activities lean can make memories better
> Note: I'm not super confidence that lean-ness is a real or useful concept. tbd.

A naive read of Kahneman's peak-end rule is that we should try to end experiences on really good notes to maximize the quality of a memory. In particular, if we feel like we've probably hit the peak of an interaction, we should end the interaction immediately to maximize the memory's valence. Now, this might sound silly, or horrible. Ultimately, it's not the right way to live. But directionally updating towards having more "**lean**" activities has some merits.

Keeping an activity lean means not diluting the experience with extraneous parts or things which are less good than the central part of the activity. It means being pretty picky about tacking extra things onto an activity. Lean activities might feel like when you have a 10 minute phone call with a friend, because of some time constraints, so you just say everything important as fast as you can. Lean activities are like having a really short and efficient meeting at work or a talk that is very to the point.

Leanness encourages keeping activities crisp and making the important parts easier to remember. Having time pressure / a deadline on an activity can cause people to make the most of an activity, to treat every moment of the activity as more precious than they o/w would. Leanness is good b/c it it thoughtful of the opp cost of activities.

Having the ability to have lean experiences can open up the possibility of having more experiences. Possibly its the case that I'd feel kind of uncomfortable / vaguely bad about calling my friend for an hour today (bc I'm busy), but I'd love talking for 10 mins. Then it's good to be able to call for 10 mins. If you intentionally make an activity lean like this, that can be extra fun, bc you can feel disciplined when you successfully cap the activity at being short.

Another nice thing about keeping activities lean is that it's a way of being direct and honest with friends. And it feels nice if your friendship is close enough that you feel comfortable and secure doing this. and you aren't worried about them thinking that you don't like spending time w/ them.

Maybe a lean activity is you end the activity on a good silly joke.

OK, no. Here's really what a lean activity is:
The feeling of a lean activity is You've just had an amazing, intense, sincere conversation with your friend. You're smiling. You don't feel a desperate need to hold onto the sands of the positive experience, the sand would just slip through your fingers. Instead you feel that it was enough, and that it was right. You feel confident and hopeful. You part ways b/c you have some understanding that poetically this moment was the right note to end the interaction on.

My overall recommendations here are:
- Maybe sometimes create lean activities on purpose in advance.
- Maybe sometimes keep activities lean rather than bloating them as you go.
## Reasons not to keep activities "short"
To balance it out, here are a few counter-considerations against keeping activities short. Note that short and lean are pretty different, but somewhat related.

Trying to leave "at the peak" of an experience can be bad for several reasons. One reason is that you can't know when you've hit the peak (altho secretary problem odds aren't so bad). Another reason is that the experiences themselves were valuable, not just the memory. It'd be terrible advice to leave a relationship at the peak time of the relationship.

Some forms of trying to optimize away bad parts of experiences can be ways of not acknowledging bad world states, or can feel like you're only contingently there for your friend.

Having lots of short interactions can be bad bc of context switching, a point in favor of having more extended activities.

Finally, I think "hanging out all day, not necc a plan, just with sureness that we enjoy spending time tgt and will think of some enjoyable way to spend the time" is a pretty nice type of experience. 
# Memory is made by telling stories about the past
Kahneman's peak-end rule is only really true on a short time scale for memories. In the longer term, the quality of our memories is purely determined by the stories we tell about the past. We can choose to emphasize or focus on whatever part we want. There isn't one objectively right way to compress a memory -- all are lossy in different ways.

My most important piece of advice here is to actually intentionally make memories. When you have an amazing experience, write it down in detail (and maybe share it with the counterparty!).

When you're having a great memory, make a mnemonic for it. Maybe you associate this shirt you're wearing with the memory. A short catchy phrase is also a pretty good way to make it more likely you remember something. 

Rehearse these memory cues. Especially via obscure references that are impossible to understand if your friend doesn't remember the relevant thing.

Remember that the above advice is supposed to be a directional update, and its possible to go too far. It's not good to live in the past too much. It's not good to hold too tightly to memories: if it's hard to hold to a memory, that's some evidence that it's not worth holding to that memory.
